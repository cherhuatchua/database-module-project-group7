## Script

### Part 1 — The question and the metric *(0:00 – 0:40)*

*(Slide: the Demand vs Competition page)*

> My part answers one question: **where are the good odds?**
>
> Finding where the jobs are is easy — count postings. Nobody measures the other half:
> **how many people are fighting for each seat?**
>
> We measured it. Our data records how many applications each posting received and how many
> seats it offered. Divide one by the other and you get a simple, powerful number:
> **applicants per seat**. One means you're practically alone. Ten means you're in a queue.

### Part 2 — The opportunity map *(0:40 – 1:40)*

*(Slide: the four-quadrant bubble map)*

> We put every job category onto this one map.
>
> - **Up** means more postings — more demand.
> - **Right** means more applicants per seat — more competition.
> - Bubble **size** is the number of open seats, and **colour** is pay.
>
> Read it as four quadrants. **Top-left is the sweet spot**: plenty of jobs, hardly any
> queue. **Top-right is the trap**: plenty of jobs, but everyone knows it.
>
> And the spread shocked us. **Competition differs by a factor of sixteen.** Personal Care
> has **0.7 applicants per seat** — fewer applicants than seats! F&B has **1.0**. But
> Social Services has **11.3** — more than eleven people per seat.
>
> Here is the headline: **Information Technology is the biggest and best-paid category —
> 140,000 postings, $6,500 median — but it sits mid-right on this map, with about 5
> applicants per seat.** Meanwhile F&B and Customer Service sit top-left: huge volume,
> almost no queue. Two categories can offer similar numbers of jobs and completely
> different chances of getting one. **No job website will ever tell you that.**

### Part 3 — The surprise finding *(1:40 – 2:30)*

*(Slide: hard-to-fill bar chart)*

> One more finding from this page, and it surprised us.
>
> Some job ads get **reposted** — the employer couldn't fill the seat the first time. You
> might guess those are elite, demanding roles. The data says the opposite.
>
> **Reposted jobs pay a median of $3,700. Jobs filled first time pay $3,850.**
>
> So hard-to-fill jobs are not elite — **they are underpaid**. The employer is struggling
> because the salary is below market. And for a job seeker, that is not a warning sign —
> that is **negotiating power**. If you see a job that keeps being reposted, you are
> allowed to ask for more.

### Part 4 — Handover *(2:30 – 2:50)*

> So this page puts the whole market on one map — with the caveat on screen at all times:
> competition comes from the reliable window, postings up to June 2023.
>
> **But a map shows everyone the same picture. How do you turn it into advice for one
> specific person? That is the Career Recommender.**

## Questions you own

**"Is 'applicants per seat' a fair measure of competition?"**
> It's the most direct one this data allows: real applications divided by real seats. It has
> limits — one person can apply to many jobs — but it compares categories on equal terms.

**"Why is the vertical axis logarithmic?"**
> Category sizes range from a few hundred postings to 140,000. On a normal axis, the small
> half of the market would be squashed flat against the bottom. We label the axis clearly.

**"Doesn't low competition just mean nobody wants those jobs?"**
> Sometimes — that's why the map shows pay as colour at the same time. Low competition plus
> low pay is a warning. Low competition plus decent pay, like Personal Care at $4,000, is an
> opportunity.

**"Why do you trust the application counts at all?"**
> Only inside the reliable window — postings up to June 2023, where the counters had time to
> accumulate. That's about 200,000 postings, which is plenty.
