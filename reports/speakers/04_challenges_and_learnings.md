## Script

### Part 1 — Three lessons *(0:00 – 0:50)*

*(Slide: three short bullets)*

> Three things this project taught us.
>
> **One — plot every number against time before you trust it.** The June 2023 break was
> invisible in summary statistics. It only appeared when we drew it — and missing it would
> have flipped our conclusions.
>
> **Two — when one row means two different things, the code must enforce the difference.**
> Our category split double-counted jobs for an hour — and the wrong number looked
> believable.
>
> **Three — a chart's job is one sentence.** Our first opportunity map labelled all 43
> categories and was unreadable. Nine labels made the point instantly.

### Part 2 — Next steps *(0:50 – 1:05)*

> Next steps: parse full job **descriptions** for skills, not just titles — and **host it
> online with saved profiles and alerts**, so the user hears when their track's score moves.

## Questions you own

**"What was the hardest part of the project?"**
> The June 2023 discovery — not finding it, but accepting what it meant: throwing 80% of the
> engagement data out of the competition metric and labelling that on every page. Honest
> numbers over impressive ones.

**"What would you do differently next time?"**
> Plot every metric against time on day one, not day three. And set up the posting-ID
> de-duplication before building any page, not after shipping a wrong number to ourselves.

**"How would this become a real product?"**
> The analytics already handle a million rows in 15 seconds. What's missing is hosting, user
> accounts, and a scheduled data refresh — engineering work, not research work.

**"Any limitations we should know about?"**
> Three, all stated in the app: salaries are advertised bands, not offers; skills are title
> keywords, not a full census; and the poster is often a recruitment agency, not the actual
> employer.
